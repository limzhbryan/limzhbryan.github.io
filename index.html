When freight spikes, who really pays for Asia's plastics?

Shipping shocks rarely land fully on the buyer. How the cost splits tells you who holds the market power.

Bryan Lim · November 2026 · 6 min read · [Sample article]

When container and tanker rates jump, the first reaction in most procurement meetings is simple: our costs just went up by the freight increase. In practice the delivered price usually rises by less than that, and the difference comes out of the producer's pocket.

How that cost is shared depends on who has options. A buyer who can switch origins forces the producer to absorb more. A producer with full order books and other markets to sell to can pass almost all of it on. Watching the gap between origin and delivered prices shows you which situation you are in.

Read the spread, not the headline

In this example, polyethylene and polypropylene producers took a large share of the hit because buyers had plenty of alternative supply. Iron ore barely moved at origin because buyers had few places to switch to. Same shock, very different outcomes.

For a buyer, this is useful in negotiation. For a producer, it is a warning about how replaceable your cargo is. Either way, the data is already in the price reports most teams subscribe to. It just needs to be read as a spread.

About

I'm an Economics PhD student at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore. I'm interested in global markets: how prices get set, how trade flows shift, and how politics changes both.

Before the PhD I spent two years at S&P Global as a price assessment specialist, covering iron ore and petrochemicals. I spoke with traders and producers across Asia every day and turned what I heard into the benchmark prices they trade on. Earlier, I worked in strategy and market research at Kantar Consulting, Jacaranda Capital Partners and FairPrice Group.

I write here to work through ideas, and to make economics useful to the people who have to make decisions with it.
